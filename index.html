Space
Settlements

In the autumn of 1969 the physicist Gerard O’Neill put a question to a small seminar of Princeton freshmen: is a planetary surface the right place for an expanding technological civilization? Over the next eight years the question grew into a body of engineering, a set of settlements built in free space from lunar material, rotating for gravity, lit by mirrors, and large enough for towns, farms and a sky. This atlas opens five of those designs so you can examine how they work, and follows the economy, energy and industry meant to pay for them.

01

Why build in free space

O’Neill’s seminar started from three observations. In space, solar energy is available all the time, with no night and no weather. Planets add surprisingly little room: settling the Moon and Mars would hardly more than double humanity’s land, and at the growth rates of the time that increase would be used up in about thirty-five years. And building material is far cheaper to lift from the Moon, whose gravity well is 22 times shallower than Earth’s.

So the land itself would be built. A rotating vessel presses its contents against its outer wall, which becomes a floor with gravity. The group considered three shapes for such a vessel, the sphere, the cylinder and the wheel, and their first calculation surprised them: a steel shell spinning for Earth gravity and loaded with soil and air could be several miles across.

In the summer of 1975, NASA’s Ames Research Center and Stanford University ran a ten-week study that worked the idea out as a complete system for 10,000 people, published in 1977 as Space Settlements: A Design Study. Its authors concluded that the obstacles were “principally philosophical, political, and social rather than technological.” O’Neill’s book The High Frontier (1976) carried the idea to a wide audience through a sequence of designs he called Island One, Two and Three.

Both works tie the settlements to an economy. The colonists refine aluminum, titanium and silicon from lunar ore and build solar power satellites, an idea Peter Glaser introduced in 1968, which send electricity to Earth. Sales of that power were meant to pay for the first colony and for the ones that followed.

02

The geography of the Earth–Moon system

Gravity shapes empty space into terrain. Earth and the Moon sit at the bottom of deep wells, and between them lie five shallow valleys where their pulls balance the rotation of the whole system, the Lagrangian libration points. Three of them are saddles that need constant station keeping. The other two, L4 and L5, lie on the Moon’s own orbit, each as far from Earth as from the Moon, and an object nudged away in any direction drifts back. The 1975 study put its colony at L5 and called the choice somewhat arbitrary, since L4 is almost identical.

03

What every settlement has to solve

Gravity by rotation

Spin presses everything against the outer wall. A larger radius allows a slower, more comfortable spin, which is why the designs are so big.

Shielding by mass

Cosmic rays are stopped by several tonnes of material over every square meter, usually lunar soil, so shielding becomes most of a settlement’s mass.

Sunlight by mirror

Mirrors carry daylight in while the structure blocks straight-line radiation, and their angles set the length of the day.

A closed loop, and heat out

Air, water and food cycle between people, crops and animals. Everything that comes in as energy leaves as heat from radiators.

Each habitat sheet in the atlas has live diagrams for these systems, scaled to that design.

04

The system around the habitats

A habitat was only one part of the 1975 plan. The rest was an industrial system reaching from a mine on the Moon to power stations over Earth, and an economy meant to pay for it all.

Topic 01 · Economy · SP-413 chapter 6

Paying for the colony

The 1975 study justified the colony with one product, solar power satellites built from lunar material and sold to Earth as electricity. Its authors admitted the risk: “it is a definite weakness of the design to depend entirely on this one particular enterprise.” Their program runs year by year from research on Earth in 1976 to a completed colony in year 22, with every figure in 1975 dollars.

What the study concluded

Annual benefits exceed costs in year 28. Over its 70-year horizon the benefit-to-cost ratio is 1.02 at a 10 percent discount rate, the federal standard of the time, and 1.5 at 8 percent. Developing a second-generation shuttle from year 3 instead of year 15 raises the ratio to 1.3 and cuts the first colony’s cost from $196.9 billion to $112.7 billion, at the price of heavier spending early.

The product

Each 10 GW power satellite earns about $1.17 billion a year sold in the US. Electricity at the busbar starts at 8.5 mils per kWh and falls to 4.8 mils by year 70, against a target of 14.1 mils set by coal and nuclear plants of 1974.

1 mil = 0.1 cent.

Learning curves

After the first, each power satellite costs about $9.73 billion, or $6.76 billion once the new shuttle flies, and each further colony about $9.24 billion. Both fall along 80 percent learning curves: every doubling of output cuts the variable cost by a fifth.

Topic 02 · Energy · SP-413 chapters 4 and 6

Power from orbit

A solar power satellite in geosynchronous orbit sees the Sun almost all the time and sends its output to the ground as a microwave beam. The idea came from Peter Glaser in 1968. The study weighed two designs, a photovoltaic one and a turbogenerator one, and judged the turbogenerator preferable because it relied on technology already in use, without making a final choice.

Capacity against demandgigawatts · program years 15–45

Capacity counts the 10 GW satellites in use from the study’s Table 6-12. US demand starts from 224 GW in 1975 and grows 5 percent a year, the study’s assumption. About a third of the power was planned for export.

How strong is the beam?

Milliwatts per square centimeter, logarithmic. Peak density at the receiving antenna is 10 to 100 mW/cm², falling to 10 µW/cm² about 10 to 15 km from the beam’s center. A fence and metal screen keep people out of the center.

Power for the settlements themselves

The torus runs on 50 MW from solar cells stretched between its spokes, 3 kW for each resident, and a 200 MW solar plant powers fabrication. The lunar base needs 200 MW, almost all of it (192 MW) for the mass driver, and two complete launchers keep shipping through the two-week lunar night. Later in the program a power satellite at L1 beams energy to the Moon on a 3 cm wavelength into a 27 km² receiving antenna.

Topic 03 · Mass driver · SP-413 chapter 5 and appendices F–I

Throwing the Moon

Ten million tonnes of lunar soil go into the first colony. The study’s mining base sits near the Apollo 16 landing site in the Cayley region, chosen for aluminum-rich ore and flat ground. Ore is compacted into small blocks, loaded into buckets with superconducting coils, and accelerated along a magnetic track at about 30 g until it leaves the Moon at 2.4 km/s, bound for a catcher at L2.

The launch trackhorizontal distances compressed; buckets run in real time

Hitting the catcher

A straight-line estimate: miss distance ≈ speed error × flight time, with the study’s fast transfers of about 48 to 77 hours. The real trajectory is more sensitive. The study held errors to 10⁻³ m/s across the path and 10⁻⁴ m/s along it.

The lunar base

A pit about 2 km across and 10 m deep, worked by scooper-loaders at about 4 t a minute. A construction crew of 300 builds the base, and 150 people run it, on two-year tours in prefabricated quarters buried under 5 m of lunar soil. Its 200 MW plant is nuclear, so launches continue through the lunar night.

The catcher

A thin net of 10 m², steered by three cables across a triangle 1 km on a side, tracks each 10 kg block by radar from 2 km out and slows it from 200 to 20 m/s. Each catcher weighs about 220 t. Five were planned for 11 million tonnes in ten years, and ore carriers then take about two months to reach L5.

Rejected: the gas gun

The study also costed a lunar gas gun firing 57 t projectiles twice an hour. It needed less equipment on the Moon, but an error of ±0.5 m/s scattered payloads over a circle about 1,000 km wide, to be rounded up by 50 interceptor rockets.
